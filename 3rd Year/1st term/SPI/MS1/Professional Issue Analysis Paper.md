# **CHAPTER I**

**INTRODUCTION AND PROBLEM FRAMING**

**A. Background of the Issue**

Auto-renewing subscriptions are now a routine way to pay for mobile software. On the Apple App Store, an auto-renewable subscription continues at the end of its period until the subscriber cancels it, and a free or discounted introductory period can end in a charge at the standard price (Apple, n.d.-b). Google Play likewise treats many in-app subscriptions as ongoing plans that renew unless the user stops them. The commercial pattern that concerns this paper is narrower than subscriptions in general. It is the free trial, or a similarly low-friction start, that becomes a recurring charge inside utility tools, photo editors, and casual games, where the screen that begins the commitment and the screen that ends it are not equally easy to understand.

Regulators and researchers describe a family of interface practices that can produce that imbalance. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC, 2022) staff report *Bringing Dark Patterns to Light* uses “dark patterns” for design practices that trick or manipulate consumers into choices they would not otherwise have made and that may cause harm. In subscription sales, the report identifies practices that hide material terms, use visual hierarchy to steer a choice, and make cancellation difficult. A 2024 sweep by the International Consumer Protection and Enforcement Network (ICPEN, 2024) looked specifically at how subscription services are marketed on websites and in mobile apps. Sweepers from 27 authorities in 26 countries examined 642 traders and reported that sneaking, including the inability to turn off auto-renewal, and interface interference, including making the option that benefits the trader more prominent, were especially common.

Those labels come from a research vocabulary as well as from enforcement. Gray et al. (2018) grouped manipulative interface strategies into nagging, obstruction, sneaking, interface interference, and forced action. Interface interference includes visual choices, such as false hierarchy, that make one option look like the natural or preferred action. Obstruction includes designs that make a user path, such as cancellation, harder than the path that starts the transaction. Di Geronimo et al. (2020) applied that vocabulary to popular free apps on Google Play and found false hierarchy among the most frequent patterns, although their count covered many kinds of dark patterns and was not limited to subscription screens.

The condition this paper examines sits at the intersection of those findings and ordinary app categories. Users of utility tools, photo editors, and casual games often meet a subscription prompt early, sometimes during the first session. The control that starts a trial can be large and high contrast, while the price, the billing period, the fact of automatic renewal, and the steps required to cancel can be smaller, lower contrast, or placed where they are easy to pass over. Apple’s own subscription guidance tells developers that the amount to be billed must be the most prominent pricing element in the purchase flow, and that a free-trial screen must state how long the trial lasts and what price follows it (Apple, n.d.-b). Google Play’s subscriptions policy tells developers that users should not have to take an extra action to see the offer terms, the cost, the billing frequency, the renewal terms, and how a trial will convert (Google, n.d.-b). The persistence of regulatory and academic attention to the same layout features indicates that the practice is a recurring feature of mobile commerce, not a single anecdotal screen.

What the available record establishes at this stage is the existence and the general shape of the practice. It does not yet establish how often the practice appears inside only utility tools, photo editors, and casual games on both stores in the current year. That limit is taken up in the evidence summary. The background here is limited to the technology and the interface condition. Stakeholder effects, professional duties, and legal rules are examined in Chapter II.

**B. Statement of the Problem**

Mobile applications distributed through the Apple App Store and Google Play, including utility tools, photo editors, and casual games, commonly offer free trials that convert into auto-renewing subscriptions. In the interfaces that present those offers, the price, the billing period, the automatic renewal, and the path required to cancel are sometimes given less visual emphasis than the control that starts the trial, and cancellation can require further steps after the user has already enrolled. The concern is that users may incur recurring charges they did not clearly understand or cannot readily stop. This is a professional issue for the user-experience designers, user-interface designers, and software developers who determine or implement that hierarchy, copy, and step sequence, because store rules and consumer-protection standards already treat clear disclosure and a workable way to stop the charge as part of the interface, not only as a feature of the business model.

**C. Scope of the Investigation**

This investigation examines auto-renewing subscriptions and free-trial conversions in mobile applications offered on the Apple App Store and Google Play, with attention to utility tools, photo editors, and casual games. The interface practices inside the boundary are the disclosure of price, duration, renewal, and cancellation, and the relative prominence and number of steps involved in accepting or stopping the commitment. The stakeholder groups kept in view are end users, the designers and developers who build the screens, the publishers who set conversion goals, Apple and Google as store operators, and consumer-protection authorities whose published guidance or enforcement describes the practice. The legal discussion uses U.S. federal consumer-protection law, principally the Restore Online Shoppers’ Confidence Act, together with the written rules of the two stores. European and international enforcement findings are used as evidence that the practice is not confined to one national market. They are not used to apply European statutes to a Philippine proceeding.

The investigation does not cover Philippine consumer statutes. It does not treat website checkout as the object of study, except where a cited study is expressly about websites and is labeled as such. Privacy-consent screens and security incidents are outside the boundary except for a short statement of why they are not the center of this problem. Children are not a separate population in this paper, even though some of the cited research discusses them. The paper does not design an interface, specify features, or propose an implementation. Those questions belong to a later recommendation stage and are not taken up in Chapters I and II.

# **CHAPTER II**

**EVIDENCE AND SOCIAL-PROFESSIONAL ANALYSIS**

**A. Evidence Summary**

The evidence is organized around four themes: what the interface practices are, how often studies find them and in which populations, whether users notice them, and where the sources agree or leave a gap. Prevalence figures are kept attached to the population that was actually studied.

The practices most relevant to this problem are visual hierarchy and cancellation friction. Gray et al. (2018) describe interface interference as a strategy that manipulates the user’s perception of the available choices, including by making one option more salient than another, and obstruction as a strategy that inserts difficulty into a path the user is trying to complete. The FTC (2022) report maps those ideas onto subscription sales. It describes sellers that bury key terms, use misleading visual hierarchy, and construct cancellation paths that are long, hard to find, or interrupted by further promotions. In one matter discussed in the report, the FTC alleged that an online learning service promised easy cancellation while requiring consumers to move through a difficult-to-find, lengthy, and confusing path. That example is an allegation in an enforcement record, not a finding about utility, photo, or game apps. It does show the kind of path the agency treats as a dark pattern in subscription commerce.

Platform documents describe the same features as duties of presentation. Before a customer subscribes, Apple Review Guideline 3.1.2(c) requires a clear description of what the user will receive for the price and points developers to Schedule 2 of the Apple Developer Program License Agreement (Apple, n.d.-a). Schedule 2, section 3.8(b), requires clear and conspicuous disclosure of the subscription title, the length of the subscription, and the price (Apple, 2024). Apple’s subscription guidance adds that the purchase screen must show the subscription name and duration, the content or services provided, and the full renewal price “clearly and prominently,” and that the amount to be billed must be the most prominent pricing element in the layout (Apple, n.d.-b). A free-trial flow must state the trial length and the price that will be billed when the trial ends. Guideline 3.1.2(a) states that apps which trick users into purchasing a subscription under false pretenses may be removed from the App Store (Apple, n.d.-a). Google Play’s subscriptions policy requires the offer terms, cost, billing-cycle frequency, automatic-renewal terms, and any trial-conversion terms to be disclosed so that users need not take an additional action to review them, and it requires an easy-to-use online method to cancel, such as a link to the Play Subscription Center or direct access to the cancellation process from account settings (Google, n.d.-b). Google’s user-facing help states a related fact that the policy alone does not make obvious: uninstalling the app does not cancel the subscription (Google, n.d.-a).

Prevalence depends on what was counted. The closest count to this paper’s topic is the ICPEN (2024) sweep of subscription marketing. Between January 29 and February 2, 2024, participating authorities reviewed the websites and mobile apps of 642 traders. ICPEN reported that 75.7% used at least one dark pattern and 66.8% used two or more, with sneaking practices such as the inability to turn off auto-renewal, and interface interference such as giving prominence to the subscription option that benefits the trader, encountered especially often. The European Commission’s (2024) questions-and-answers note on the Digital Fairness Fitness Check reports a consumer survey in which 40% of respondents considered that the design of the website or app made cancellation very difficult, and 29% reported that a free trial was often extended automatically into a paid subscription. The same note cites a 2022 Commission study in which 97% of the most popular websites and apps used by consumers in the European Union deployed at least one dark pattern. The most common patterns in that study included hiding information, false hierarchies, repeated requests, and difficult cancellations. These figures describe subscription marketing and popular European sites and apps. They are not a census of utility tools, photo editors, and casual games.

Mobile-app research supplies a second, wider count that must not be collapsed into the first. Di Geronimo et al. (2020) manually reviewed 240 popular free applications from the U.S. Google Play store, 30 in each of eight categories, and recorded the first ten minutes of use. They reported that 95% contained at least one dark pattern and that popular apps included, on average, at least seven types. Nagging, false hierarchy, and preselection were among the most common. The authors state that they classified the finished interface and did not try to decide whether a designer intended the result. They also limit the finding: the sample was free apps, the observation window was the first ten minutes, and paid apps or later screens might differ. False hierarchy in that study is evidence that salient-versus-subordinate choices are common in popular Play apps. It is not evidence that 95% of those apps hide subscription prices.

Website research is relevant only as a comparison, and it is labeled as such. Mathur et al. (2019) crawled about 11,000 shopping websites and found dark patterns on about 11% of them. The types included sneaking practices such as hidden subscriptions and obstruction practices such as flows that are hard to cancel. Di Geronimo et al. (2020) cite that work as evidence from e-commerce websites, not from mobile apps. A shopping-site rate of about 11% and a free-app rate of 95% are not in conflict once the populations and the definitions are kept apart. The website crawl looked for specified commerce patterns across a large set of sites. The app study looked for a broad dark-pattern taxonomy in a smaller set of popular free apps. Neither number is the rate of unclear trial terms inside this paper’s three app categories.

On recognition, Di Geronimo et al. (2020) ran an online experiment with 589 participants who watched short videos of app interfaces. For the videos that contained a dark pattern, 55% of responses did not identify a malicious design, 20% were unsure, and 25% reported one. Participants were more likely to notice a pattern after they had already been asked about malicious design, and participants with prior knowledge of the topic performed better. The sample was more highly educated than the general population of app users, and watching a thirty-second video is not the same as using an app. The result still supports a limited claim: many users do not notice the patterns researchers had already classified, at least on a first look and without being primed. The FTC (2022) and the European Commission (2024) treat the consumer consequence as unexpected or hard-to-stop recurring charges. That consequence is consistent with a recognition gap, but the perception study does not itself measure money lost.

The sources agree on the character of the practice and disagree, or simply differ, on its measured frequency. The FTC (2022), ICPEN (2024), and the European Commission (2024) all treat hidden or subordinated terms and difficult cancellation as recurring features of subscription interfaces, including mobile apps. Apple (n.d.-a, n.d.-b, 2024) and Google (n.d.-b) state, in operational language, that price, duration, renewal, and a practicable way to cancel must be clear. Gray et al. (2018) and Di Geronimo et al. (2020) supply the design categories, especially false hierarchy and obstruction, that make those regulatory descriptions precise. The disagreement is not about whether a large accept control and a buried cancellation path can mislead. It is about how widely that pairing occurs. A subscription-specific international sweep finds it in a large majority of reviewed traders (ICPEN, 2024). A broad mobile study finds dark patterns in nearly all sampled free apps, but not subscription dark patterns specifically (Di Geronimo et al., 2020). A website crawl finds commerce dark patterns on a minority of shopping sites (Mathur et al., 2019).

What remains uncertain is the rate inside the exact boundary of this paper. No source reviewed here counts only current utility, photo-editor, and casual-game trial and cancellation screens on both the App Store and Google Play. Store rules show what presentation is required. They do not, by themselves, prove that a particular app has breached them. The team’s reading of the combined record is that the practice is documented often enough, and in the right commercial setting, to justify investigating it as a professional issue of interface design. That reading is an inference from several populations. It is not a new measurement.

**B. Stakeholders and Impacts**

End users are the people who see the trial screen, accept or decline it, and later try to stop a renewal. The harm supported by the regulatory record is a recurring charge that was poorly disclosed or difficult to end, together with the time spent finding the cancellation path (European Commission, 2024; FTC, 2022; ICPEN, 2024). Users are not affected in one way. A person who reads subordinate text, who already knows that uninstalling a Play app leaves the subscription in place (Google, n.d.-a), or who manages renewals from the store’s subscription list can avoid or limit the charge. Di Geronimo et al. (2020) found that prior knowledge improved recognition and that most unprimed viewers did not spot the pattern in the video. The impact is therefore uneven: it falls more heavily on users who take the prominent control as the whole offer. This paper does not claim a special measured effect for older users or for users with lower digital literacy, because the sources assembled here do not establish that group difference.

User-experience designers, user-interface designers, and software developers are the professionals who decide or implement type size, contrast, control emphasis, the wording of the trial, and the number of steps in cancellation. Di Geronimo et al. (2020) note that a finished pattern can result from a mistake, from management pressure, or from copying a popular interface, and that their classification did not require proof of intent. That distinction matters. A developer who inherits a paywall, or a designer who is asked to raise the trial-start rate, may not have chosen the subscription model and may still be the person who makes the accept control larger than the price. Apple’s guidance itself holds both aims in view: it tells developers to keep the purchase flow short because a long sign-up lowers conversion, and it tells them that the billed amount must remain the most prominent price (Apple, n.d.-b). The professional impact is a responsibility for the layout even when the revenue target is set elsewhere, and a risk that “keep the flow short” will be implemented as “make the terms easy to miss.”

App publishers, including product and commercial teams, are a separate group because they receive the benefit the interface is built to produce. A trial that converts, and a subscription that continues because cancellation is easy to postpone, is revenue. Publishers also carry the store-policy and legal exposure if the screen violates a disclosure rule (Apple, n.d.-a; Google, n.d.-b). Their interest in conversion is legitimate as a business goal. It becomes part of this problem when that goal is pursued by subordinating material terms or by lengthening the path that stops payment. Not every publisher uses those tactics. ICPEN’s (2024) figures, high as they are, still leave a minority of reviewed traders with no detected pattern.

Apple and Google occupy two roles at once. They write the rules that require clear price, duration, renewal, and cancellation (Apple, 2024, n.d.-a, n.d.-b; Google, n.d.-b), and they review or can remove apps that trick users into a subscription (Apple, n.d.-a). They also host the account tools through which many subscriptions are actually stopped. On Google Play, cancellation from the store can be the practical path, and removing the app is not that path (Google, n.d.-a). The impact on the platforms is reputational and operational: their marketplaces are the distribution channel in which the pattern appears, and their policies are the professional standard designers are expected to meet. Their rules are evidence of a duty. Compliance review is not evidence that every listed app meets the duty.

Consumer-protection authorities, including the FTC and the authorities participating in the ICPEN sweep, do not draw the buttons. They document the practice, bring or coordinate cases, and state what disclosure and cancellation should look like (European Commission, 2024; FTC, 2022; ICPEN, 2024). Their influence is indirect and uneven across countries. A rule or a staff report can change what counts as an acceptable screen without changing any one app until a publisher, a reviewer, or a court acts. Users in markets with active enforcement and users in markets with only platform rules do not face the same external check. This paper uses those authorities as sources of evidence and of legal duty. It does not treat any of them as the designer of the interface.

**C. Ethical and Professional Analysis**

The ethical tension is not that an auto-renewing subscription is inherently wrongful. A trial can be a fair way to let someone try a photo editor, a utility, or a game, and the renewal can be the means of paying for continued access. Apple (n.d.-b) describes that model as appropriate where the app continues to provide value. The tension that the evidence supports is an asymmetry inside the interface: the action that starts the charge is easier to see and complete than the information needed to understand the charge or the action that stops it (Apple, n.d.-b; FTC, 2022; Google, n.d.-b; Gray et al., 2018; ICPEN, 2024). When that asymmetry is large, consent to the renewal is thinner than a tap on the prominent control suggests. The team treats that as a problem of informed choice, not as proof that every designer intended to deceive.

The Association for Computing Machinery (ACM, 2018) Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct gives a way to evaluate that asymmetry without turning the code into a list of slogans. Principle 1.3, “Be honest and trustworthy,” requires computing professionals to be transparent and to provide full disclosure of pertinent system capabilities, limitations, and potential problems, and it treats deliberately false or misleading claims as a violation. A subscription screen that states a weekly price in large type and the annual amount that will actually be billed in smaller type is in tension with that principle, and it is also in tension with Apple’s (n.d.-b) instruction that the billed amount be the most prominent pricing element. Principle 1.2, “Avoid harm,” defines harm to include unjustified negative consequences, including damage to property. An unexpected recurring charge is a financial harm of that kind when the interface, rather than the user’s informed refusal to cancel, is what explains the charge. The principle also recognizes that harm can follow from actions that were not aimed at harm. That clause fits Di Geronimo et al.’s (2020) observation that a pattern may come from copying or from schedule pressure rather than from a plan to mislead. Avoiding harm still applies: the professional obligation does not wait for proof of motive.

Principle 2.5 calls for comprehensive and thorough evaluations of computer systems and their impacts, including possible risks. Applied here, the evaluation is not only whether the trial button is easy to tap. It includes whether a user can learn the price, the period, and the renewal before paying, and whether the cancellation path is realistic, including the fact that deleting the app may leave a Play subscription running (Google, n.d.-a). Principle 3.1 states that the public good is the central concern of professional computing work and that people affected by the system, including users, should remain the central concern. A conversion metric can be a legitimate project goal. Under 3.1 it is not the only goal that counts when the metric is improved by making material terms less noticeable.

The word “dark pattern” needs the same care. Gray et al. (2018) and Di Geronimo et al. (2020) use it for interfaces that benefit the service at the expense of a choice the user might not have made if fully informed. Di Geronimo et al. also record borderline cases and say that some screens can be read differently by different reviewers. A highlighted subscribe button, by itself, is ordinary visual design. It becomes the practice at issue when prominence, omission, or extra steps obscure the price, the renewal, or the way out. Using the research term for that narrower practice avoids treating every paywall as unethical.

The tradeoff should be stated plainly. Shortening a sign-up can respect the user’s time, and Apple (n.d.-b) warns that a long purchase flow reduces conversion. Funding a free utility or game through an optional subscription can be compatible with user benefit. The competing responsibility is that brevity and visual emphasis are the same tools that hide a renewal. A designer or developer can satisfy both only by keeping the flow short and still giving the billed amount, the period, and the cancellation route enough prominence that a first-time user can act on them. Where product requirements forbid that balance, the code does not make the conflict disappear. It identifies the professional cost of resolving the conflict only in favor of conversion.

**D. Relevant Legal, Privacy, and Security Considerations**

The legal frame for this investigation is U.S. federal consumer-protection law for online negative-option sales, read together with the contractual rules of the two stores. It is not a determination that any named application has violated the law. The course task and the evidence do not support that kind of judgment.

For transactions on the internet that use a negative-option feature, the Restore Online Shoppers’ Confidence Act, 15 U.S.C. § 8403 (2010), makes it unlawful to charge a consumer unless three conditions are met. The seller must clearly and conspicuously disclose all material terms before obtaining billing information. The seller must obtain the consumer’s express informed consent before charging the consumer’s account. The seller must provide simple mechanisms for the consumer to stop recurring charges. A free trial that renews into a paid subscription unless the user cancels is a negative-option structure of this kind. The statute therefore speaks directly to the two interface facts in the problem statement: what is disclosed before the charge, and how simple it is to stop later charges. The FTC (2022) staff report is not itself a statute. It explains how the Commission, under its authority over unfair or deceptive acts or practices, has analyzed dark patterns in subscription sales, including buried terms and difficult cancellation.

A more specific federal rule should not be described as currently in force. In 2024 the FTC amended its negative-option rule in a form commonly called click-to-cancel. On July 8, 2025, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Eighth Circuit vacated that amended rule. The Commission’s subsequent advance notice of proposed rulemaking, published on March 13, 2026, records that vacatur and asks for comment on whether new negative-option requirements are needed (Federal Trade Commission, 2026). The legal baseline argued in this paper is therefore the 2010 statute and the Commission’s existing deceptive-practice authority, not the vacated amendments. The open rulemaking is a source of uncertainty: the policy direction is still contested, even though the underlying complaint about recurring charges is not new.

Store rules are professional and contractual requirements for distribution, not substitutes for the statute. Apple Review Guideline 3.1.2(c), Schedule 2’s disclosure list, the prohibition on tricking users into a subscription, and the prominence rule for the billed amount set the App Store standard (Apple, 2024, n.d.-a, n.d.-b). Google Play’s subscriptions policy sets the corresponding Play standard for visible terms and an in-app route to cancellation (Google, n.d.-b). A screen can raise a professional-compliance question under those documents even when no public enforcement order names the app. State automatic-renewal laws in the United States add further variation, but this paper does not survey them. The existence of that patchwork is noted only to avoid implying that ROSCA is the only domestic rule a publisher may face.

Privacy and security are adjacent to payment and account systems, and they are not the problem under investigation. Subscription flows collect or confirm account and payment data, and some dark-pattern research, including Di Geronimo et al. (2020), documents separate patterns that steer users into broader data sharing. Those patterns matter, and they are a different issue from the clarity of price, renewal, and cancellation. This paper does not analyze consent to tracking, secondary use of personal data, or a duty under a data-protection statute. Security failures, such as account takeover or payment-data breach, would change the risk to subscribers, but no source used here ties this interface problem to a security incident. Unless later evidence makes that link, privacy and security remain boundaries rather than findings.

The legal and professional materials shape the problem in one respect that later recommendations would have to respect. Any response has to address both ends of the subscription: the screen on which the user accepts a trial, and the mechanism by which the user stops the renewal, including store-level cancellation that survives deletion of the app (Google, n.d.-a; Restore Online Shoppers’ Confidence Act, 2010). The evidence does not identify a single layout that satisfies every store and every enforcement authority. It does identify the disclosures and the cancellation simplicity that those authorities already treat as required.
